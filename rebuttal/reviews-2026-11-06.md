## Reviewer T29u (02 Nov 2026)

Paper Summary: This paper introduces RECLAIM, a benchmark of 100 NeurIPS 2025 papers for evaluating whether autonomous agents can reproduce a paper's central empirical claim from the artifacts its authors released. The authors evaluate four agents once per paper, yielding 400 agent-paper cells. The best agent reproduces 41% of Run-tier, 27% of Retrain-tier, and 15% of Reimplement-tier papers, with 73/400 reproductions overall. Most runs use far less compute than granted, and the failure analysis identifies unvalidated reimplementation, wrong-artifact measurement, and early stopping as major failure modes.

Critical Strengths: The benchmark targets a real and important bottleneck in autonomous research. Unlike many agent benchmarks that provide a prepared environment or a fixed verifier, RECLAIM asks agents to reproduce a published claim under realistic artifact-availability conditions. The tiering by released code, data, and weights is natural and directly measures a capability relevant to autonomous research agents.

The evaluation and diagnostic design is unusually careful. Grading from execution evidence rather than agent self-reports, applying anti-cheat flags, freezing targets and rubrics before evaluation, and providing run-level failure analyses substantially strengthen the empirical contribution.

Critical Weaknesses: The reported rates are point estimates from a single run per agent-paper cell. With four agents, one venue/year, and one rollout per cell, run-to-run stochasticity and environment sensitivity could materially affect both agent rankings and tier-level comparisons.

The central measurement relies heavily on an LLM auditor. The reported F1 of 0.82 against human graders is encouraging, but the paper would benefit from a more detailed audit of disagreements, especially near the reproduced/partial boundary and for high-severity integrity flags.

Questions For Authors:
1. Can you provide a confusion matrix between the LLM auditor and human graders, especially for scores near the 7/8 boundary and for high-severity anti-cheat flags?
2. How sensitive are the conclusions to the choice of match target and the default 5% tolerance when the paper does not state one?

## Reviewer rqBN (19 Oct 2026)

Paper Summary: The paper introduces RECLAIM, a benchmark of 100 NeurIPS 2025 papers in which an autonomous agent must reproduce one pre-specified empirical claim using the paper and whatever artifacts its authors released.

Critical Strengths: The methodological disclosure is excellent. The paper provides prompts, rubric text, model-serving configurations, task tables, compute estimates, run traces, and detailed vignettes. The distinction among faithful misses, unvalidated reimplementations, wrong experiments, stale results, and dependency failures is more actionable than a single binary leaderboard.

Critical Weaknesses: Evaluator and evidence validity are also material concerns. Claude Sonnet 5 obtains F1 = 0.82 against one human label per run, but the paper omits the confusion matrix, precision, recall, tier-conditioned errors, multi-human adjudication, and validation of the full 0-10 scale and anti-cheat flags. More seriously, commands.log and trajectory.jsonl are writable by the evaluated agent without an integrity check. An instruction to distrust them does not replace an external append-only or tamper-evident event log.

Questions For Authors:
1. Can the authors provide a stratified human-expert reproduction baseline across Run, Retrain, and Reimplement using the same environment and budgets?
2. How were central claims, minimal configurations, target tuples, and tolerances independently validated, and what was the agreement among annotators?

## Reviewer YGTR (11 Oct 2026)

Paper Summary: This paper introduces Reproducing Claims in Machine Learning (RECLAIM), which evaluates AI agents on whether they can reproduce released empirical results of NeurIPS 2025 papers. It is extendable to other papers. The work distinguishes between run-tier releases (code, data, weights released), retrain-tier releases (code, data), and reimplement-tier releases (data only) with different results for each. One of the findings suggests that a common failure mode is the agents rewriting codes without comparing results against those in the actual papers.

Critical Strengths: The task and evaluation design is sound, explained in detail and reusable.

Critical Weaknesses: No frontier models are tested and the agentic harness choice is questionable. The tested models are rather weak, so it is not obvious that any claimed findings/results would generalize. Moreover, the React harness is very minimal, especially for such long paper reproduction tasks. A better setup would be to use better agentic harnesses like ClaudeCode, Codex or miniSWE-agent, which would make the setup more realistic and relevant in practice. A researcher asking AI to reproduce AI/ML paper results would ask a powerful coding agent, not set up a simple React harness to do so. The corresponding success/failure modes would change significantly if you used a better harness.

Not thorough enough analysis of confounding factors. I suspect the analysis includes a number of confounding factors, e.g. it's not obvious how many of the failures are due to the hardware differences, rather than the fault of the agent. Agents are also only run once so it's not clear whether some failures were transient or are actually consistent.

Questions For Authors:
1. Can you repeat the analysis with stronger frontier models and proper coding harnesses?
2. Given the network access, did you check whether the agents have "cheated" by e.g. fetching model checkpoints, rather than training from scratch?
3. Have you checked how many failures were not actually the agent's fault?
